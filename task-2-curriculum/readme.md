# Task 2: Curriculum Audit and 2030 Readiness

**HKAIVERSE × Leanova Assessment**

## 1. Introduction

This task examines how a B.Tech curriculum in Artificial Intelligence and Data Science could evolve to prepare students for changing AI and software engineering requirements.

AI engineering increasingly involves more than understanding algorithms or training machine learning models. Practical work may also require data preparation, software development, testing, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, security, documentation, and responsible system design.

The objective is to review curriculum priorities, identify areas for improvement, and propose a practical redesign that preserves essential mathematical and computing foundations while strengthening applied engineering capabilities.

The report presents a hypothetical curriculum-redesign proposal for academic discussion.

## 2. Objectives

The main objectives are to:

1. Review curriculum priorities and identify potential areas for improvement.
2. Classify recommendations using KEEP, UPDATE, REDUCE, and ADD.
3. Compare curriculum priorities with relevant AI and software engineering role requirements.
4. Design AI-assisted learning exercises that include independent verification.
5. Explore a hypothetical credit-impact scenario.
6. Propose student exchange and collaborative learning opportunities.
7. Design an integrated AI systems capstone based on the mentor–parent communication concept.
8. Recommend an implementation and evaluation approach.

## 3. Curriculum Review Framework

The assessment uses four categories to organize its recommendations.

| Category | Meaning | Intended purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **KEEP** | Retain essential subjects and learning outcomes | Preserve mathematical, algorithmic, and computing foundations |
| **UPDATE** | Refresh existing content, labs, or assessments | Connect theoretical knowledge with practical engineering |
| **REDUCE** | Consider reducing duplicated or low-value content after detailed review | Make room for deeper learning without unnecessary repetition |
| **ADD** | Introduce new practical capabilities or integrated learning opportunities | Address potential gaps in applied AI engineering |

A REDUCE recommendation does not automatically mean that a subject should be removed. Any reduction would require detailed syllabus comparison and institutional review.

## 4. Curriculum Priorities

The proposed redesign emphasizes the relationship between foundational knowledge and practical application.

### Programming and Algorithms

Programming, data structures, and algorithms remain essential for problem-solving, performance analysis, and software development.

Potential improvements include more code review, boundary testing, debugging, algorithm comparison, and explanations of implementation trade-offs.

### Databases and Data Engineering

Database knowledge and data preparation are important for building reliable AI applications.

Potential improvements include SQL practice, data validation, missing-value analysis, reproducible data pipelines, data quality checks, and privacy-aware handling.

### Machine Learning and Deep Learning

Students should understand model fundamentals and demonstrate that they can evaluate models correctly.

Suggested practical work includes baseline comparisons, train/validation/test separation, data leakage checks, error analysis, metric selection, and reproducible experiments.

### Software Systems and Deployment

AI models often need to operate inside a larger software system.

Potential additions include API integration, automated testing, configuration management, access control, logging, monitoring, error handling, and safe recovery.

### Generative AI and Responsible AI

GenAI learning should extend beyond prompt writing to include grounding, evaluation, privacy, hallucination testing, human review, and failure analysis.

Students should learn to verify AI-generated output rather than treating it as automatically correct.

## 5. Industry Alignment

The report considers three broad role profiles to explore the capabilities that a future-facing curriculum should support.

| Role profile | Important capabilities | Curriculum implications |
|---|---|---|
| Applied Scientist / ML Intern | Python, statistics, ML evaluation, experiment design | Preserve theory and strengthen reproducibility and model evaluation |
| AI / Software Engineer Intern | Algorithms, APIs, databases, testing, deployment | Integrate software quality and deployment practices into practical work |
| Data / Analytics Engineer Intern | SQL, data quality, pipelines, visualization, privacy | Strengthen data validation and end-to-end analytical workflows |

These are hypothetical role profiles used for curriculum planning, not claims about specific current vacancies or employers' exact requirements.

The central recommendation is to evaluate students on whether they can apply their knowledge, explain technical decisions, validate outputs, and build reliable systems.

## 6. AI-Assisted Learning Exercises

The assessment proposes five learning exercises designed to combine AI assistance with independent verification.

### Exercise 1: Data Structures and Algorithms

Use AI to help explain an algorithm such as binary search. Verify its complexity, preconditions, boundary cases, and correctness using test cases.

### Exercise 2: Database Design

Use AI to suggest a relational schema for a student enrollment system. Check keys, relationships, normalization, constraints, and duplicate records.

### Exercise 3: Machine Learning Evaluation

Use AI to suggest suitable metrics for an imbalanced classification problem. Compare the recommendation against a baseline and examine data leakage and evaluation methodology.

### Exercise 4: Natural Language Processing

Use AI to draft a concise summary from provided notes. Check each statement against the source and remove unsupported claims or unnecessary personal information.

### Exercise 5: GenAI Workflow Design

Use AI to suggest a workflow incorporating structured output, retrieval, audit logging, and human escalation. Evaluate its proposed design against failure cases and low-confidence inputs.

The report uses illustrative responses and verification decisions to demonstrate the evaluation method. These examples are not represented as live AI experiments.

## 7. Hypothetical Credit-Impact Analysis

The report uses a provisional total of 166 credits for scenario planning.

Under that assumption:

- 20% of the total corresponds to 33.2 credits.
- 25% corresponds to 41.5 credits.
- A hypothetical redesign affecting 36 credits represents approximately 21.7% of the assumed total.

The scenario considers updating practical assessments, integrating software testing and deployment, strengthening responsible AI, and improving project-based evaluation.

The preferred approach is to redesign or reallocate existing learning activities where appropriate, rather than automatically increasing the total graduation-credit requirement.

**Important:** These figures are illustrative planning values, not a verified calculation against an official batch-specific curriculum or an approved university change.

## 8. Student Exchange and Collaborative Learning

International collaboration could help students gain experience working with different teams, project expectations, and approaches to AI engineering.

The proposed pathway includes:

- Virtual collaborative projects.
- Joint AI systems development.
- Short summer research or project opportunities.
- Potential semester exchange, subject to eligibility and approval.

A hypothetical six-week virtual project could involve shared requirements, team-based development, code review, regular demonstrations, and a final presentation.

Possible evaluation measures include project completion, rubric scores, workload, collaboration quality, and documented learning outcomes.

These are proposed opportunities, not confirmed institutional partnerships or approved exchange arrangements.

## 9. Integrated AI Systems Capstone

The report uses the AI-assisted mentor–parent communication concept from Task 1 as a capstone case study.

The proposed capstone connects multiple learning areas in one applied project.

### Core components

1. **Requirements analysis:** Document the problem, intended users, workflow, and success criteria.
2. **Data handling:** Use synthetic records and validate required fields.
3. **Backend development:** Design APIs and structured workflow processing.
4. **AI integration:** Explore summarization or information preparation with appropriate checks.
5. **Human approval:** Prevent consequential actions from being taken without the required authorization.
6. **Testing:** Cover normal inputs, missing information, ambiguous output, duplicate requests, and service failures.
7. **Security and auditability:** Apply access restrictions and appropriate logging.
8. **Evaluation:** Compare the proposed system against a defined baseline and report limitations.

### Expected learning outcomes

Students should be able to translate a workflow problem into requirements, implement a small system, verify its behavior, explain AI limitations, apply appropriate safeguards, and communicate the results.

The capstone is a proposed learning design, not a claim that a completed institutional pilot has been conducted.

## 10. Implementation Roadmap

The proposed redesign follows a staged approach.

**Stage 1 — Update existing practical work**

Introduce stronger testing, documentation, data validation, and reproducibility into relevant labs.

**Stage 2 — Develop an integrated project**

Use a defined AI systems problem to connect software engineering, data handling, AI evaluation, and human oversight.

**Stage 3 — Conduct a controlled pilot**

Use synthetic data and agreed assessment rubrics to evaluate the learning experience.

**Stage 4 — Evaluate outcomes**

Measure practical performance, testing quality, reproducibility, workload, and student feedback.

**Stage 5 — Consider broader adoption**

Expand the approach only after reviewing learning outcomes, feasibility, credit implications, and institutional requirements.

This is a proposed implementation sequence, not an approved institutional timetable.

## 11. Evaluation and Limitations

The assessment uses a structured curriculum framework, hypothetical role profiles, illustrative AI exercises, and a scenario-based credit calculation.

The recommendations should be interpreted in light of these limitations:

- The hypothetical course and credit assumptions are not a substitute for an official batch-specific curriculum.
- The role profiles are not actual job descriptions.
- The AI exercise examples are not live experimental results.
- The proposed stakeholder perspectives are not actual interview findings.
- Student exchange opportunities require partner identification and institutional approval.
- Any formal curriculum change requires appropriate academic review.

These limitations do not prevent the proposal from outlining a possible direction for curriculum improvement, but they must be considered before implementation.

## 12. Expected Outcomes

If developed and evaluated successfully, the proposed redesign could help students:

- Strengthen their practical AI engineering capabilities.
- Connect theoretical concepts with complete software workflows.
- Develop better testing and verification habits.
- Understand responsible AI and system limitations.
- Build integrated projects that demonstrate applied skills.
- Improve technical documentation and communication.
- Gain experience with collaborative and industry-oriented work.

These are intended learning outcomes, not measured results from an implemented redesign.

## 13. Conclusion

Preparing AI and Data Science graduates for 2030 requires a balance between strong foundations and practical engineering ability.

The proposed curriculum direction retains essential theory while strengthening software quality, reproducible AI evaluation, responsible AI, deployment practices, and integrated project work. The capstone and exchange pathway provide ways to connect academic learning with collaborative, applied experience.

The recommendations offer a structured starting point for future curriculum review, with formal adoption dependent on detailed curriculum mapping, feasibility assessment, and institutional approval.
